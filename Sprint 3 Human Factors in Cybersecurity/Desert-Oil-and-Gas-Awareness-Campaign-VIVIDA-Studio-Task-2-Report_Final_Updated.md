**Desert Oil and Gas Awareness Campaign - VIVIDA Studio Task 2 Report**

# **Introduction**

This report covers Task 2 of Sprint 3 (Human Factors in Cybersecurity): building a security awareness campaign in VIVIDA Studio. The task is not just to generate AI content. It is to show a real grasp of the human factor risks in the chosen industry, and to get those risks across to the people inside the organization in a way that is clear and that sticks.

The organization is **Desert Oil and Gas**; the campaign focuses on the human factor risk that opens the most doors in this sector: **phishing and social engineering**.

The story spine is a fake Eid bonus email that pressures a busy manager into one click, and the behaviour the campaign drives is one habit that defeats almost every version of the attack: **Not sure? Ask first.**

This Campaign includes the prompts used to create each asset, the tweaks and edits applied while iterating, and the challenges met in the Studio along with how each was solved. All five required deliverable types were produced and reviewed for message, tone, spelling and brand consistency:

-   **5 image posts** (unique visuals carrying the campaign messages)
-   **6 text posts** (short written posts for Slack or Teams)
-   **2 video reels** (short animated clips, under the 6 to 10 second limit)
-   **1 graphic** (a branded infographic: the attack chain)
-   **1 interactive story** (the audience makes a decision and sees the consequence)

# **Overview**

The campaign is built on a single, consistent world called the **Desert Oil and Gas Campaign**. Every asset shares the same characters, locations, visual language and security message, so the audience sees one coherent story rather than a set of disconnected posts. This section summarizes that world. The Findings section then records how each asset was produced, the exact prompts used, the tweaks applied, and the challenges met.

## **The human factor risk and the narrative spine**

Attackers rarely break the technology first. They target people: trust, helpfulness, and the pressure of a busy day.  
  
The campaign dramatizes this. A patient attacker watches the organization, learns who is trusted and when they are busiest, then sends a message that looks like it comes from someone the target knows, timed for the moment the target is most hurried. One click at a desk in the Dhahran headquarters ends up as a safety and operations incident at a remote production site. The counter story is just as simple: a colleague who pauses, checks the sender and reports the message stops the whole attack for everyone.

## **Cast**

The characters are deliberately ordinary and relatable. The attackers are not cartoon hackers. They look like slightly off everyday people, and their schemes fail the moment someone asks a question.

-   **Fahad** - early 40s, mid-level Operations Manager at the Dhahran headquarters. Overworked and dedicated, he approves many requests a day and helps first. Never careless, only busy. He is the phishing target and the player character.
-   **Leila** - Head of Cyber Security. The defender who can block a reported email for the whole company.
-   **Althea Santos** - 25, security analyst. She sets up the controls and explains why they matter, asks questions, and never makes anyone feel silly for not knowing.
-   **Tariq** - 38, field technician in the Ras Tanura production unit. He relies on the systems Fahad manages, so he feels a compromise first.
-   **Farida** - late 30s staff member, part of the diverse everyday cast.
-   **The Ghost** - 40s, the patient, state sponsored watcher. Pale and semi-transparent with a soft outline, plain simple clothes, face never fully shown. Deliberately not a hacker cliche: no hood, no hat or coat, no keyboard, no green code.
-   **The Mirage** - the attacker who handles the disguises and sends the messages while The Ghost watches. Calm and sly, with a faint heat haze outline.

## **Locations**

-   **Corporate Office, Dhahran** - modern open plan headquarters floor with email and an intranet. Where the click happens.
-   **Production Unit, Ras Tanura** - remote coastal refinery with SCADA systems, pipelines and physical pressure valves. Where the consequence lands.
-   **Managerial office and IT operations room** - Fahad's busy desk, plus a walk up help desk with the sign "Ask us anything" and a speech bubble question mark icon.
-   **Server Room** - quiet rack room with status lights and an "All systems normal" banner, used in the consequence reel.
-   **Refinery Lobby and Control Plant Floor** - supporting empty sets.

## **Visual style and accessibility**

The look is a clean, flat 3D illustration with soft shading, like a modern safety manual, with a fixed palette on every asset: dark green #1B4332 and dark blue #12355B for backgrounds, warm white #FAF7F0 for text on them, and charcoal #1F2933 for text on light surfaces. Accessibility was designed in, in line with Task 3:

-   High contrast between text and background on every asset.
-   Colour is never used alone to carry meaning. Status is always shown with text or an icon as well as colour.
-   A recurring speech bubble question mark icon marks the "ask" moment in every asset.
-   No hacker cliches (no green code rain, no hooded figures at keyboards).

## **Step 3: Text posts**

Six short text posts for Slack or Teams, each built through the VIVIDA text post wizard: Risk, then Audience, then the Ask, then Where it lands, then a Review step where a steering note is added before the post is written. Each post carries one angle, stays under roughly 60 words, keeps a warm and blame free tone, and reinforces "Not sure? Ask first." The Studio choices and the steering note used for each post are recorded below.

**Tweak applied to every post:** VIVIDA inserts a source reference chip (a code in square brackets) into each draft. Each chip was removed in "Edit the wording", deleting only the bracket and its single adjacent space so no neighbouring word or punctuation was clipped. Drafts were then read once for grammar, since removing a chip can leave a run on or a missing full stop.

**How to create the text posts**

Every text post was built the same way, through the VIVIDA text-post wizard. The click path is:

1\. On the dashboard, click **Create** on the left side.

2\. Choose **Text Post**.

3\. The page asks **"What's the Risk?"** Select the risk for this post and click **Continue**.

4\. The page asks **"Who is it for?"** Select the audience and click **Continue**.

5\. The page asks **"What should they do?"** Pick one or two listed behaviours, or type your own in the **"Or write the ask yourself..."** box and click **+Add**. Then click **Continue**.

6\. The page asks **"Where will they read it?"** Select **Slack or Teams**.

7\. On the **"Ready to write it"** page, type the steering note into the **"Anything to add (optional)"** box, then click **Write the Post**.

8\. Review the draft. In **Edit the wording**, delete the source-reference chip (the code in square brackets), then re-read for any run-on or missing full stop.

9\. When the post reads correctly, click **Approve**.

# **Recommendations to Improve Vivida Studio**

**Context**

Recommendations for improving VIVIDA Studio, drawn from producing the Desert Oil and Gas awareness campaign.  
  
The campaign produced five image posts, six text posts, two reels, one graphic and one interactive story, all inside a single VIVIDA world.  

They are ordered by how much friction each one removes. Every item here is something we actually ran into while building the campaign.

**On-screen text rendering (the biggest pain point)**

This caused more rerolls than everything else combined: the email header, the lockout text, the lookalike address, the word "shutdown", and the brand name all garbled or duplicated.

1.  **A real text layer, not AI-generated pixels.** Let users type the exact text that must appear on a screen, sign or label, and render it as a crisp vector overlay on top of the generation. The model places and styles the box; the letters come from the typed string. This one feature would remove most of the garbling.
2.  **A verbatim text field (spell-lock).** For text the model does draw, a field marked "render exactly" that it is penalized for altering. We had to spell the address out character by character and still fought duplication ("Mem Memo", "Deserttpment Oin").
3.  **A readability warning.** Flag when requested on-screen text is below the size the Studio renders reliably, and suggest reframing, such as a straight-on close-up insert.

**Character and brand consistency**

1.  **Stronger reference-locking.** "Use this character reference" should be near-absolute. The Ghost kept rendering with a hat despite an explicit no-hat rule in his sheet. Make the lock a hard constraint, not a suggestion.
2.  **Project-level negative prompts.** Set campaign-wide "never include" rules once (no hats on the Ghost, no green code, one spelling of the domain) instead of pasting them into every asset.
3.  **A palette lock per world** so every asset inherits the exact hex palette automatically.

**Editing safety and history**

1.  **Undo and version history.** Interactive-story slides were lost to an accidental delete with no way back. Basic undo and auto-saved versions are table stakes.
2.  **Duplicate-before-edit (non-destructive edits)** so a regeneration never silently replaces the only copy.
3.  **Prompt history per asset.** We could not retrieve the exact prompt used for the infographic later. Every asset should store the prompts that made it, viewable and copyable.  
      
      
      
    

**Workflow and UX**

1.  **Clearer content status.** Approved versus "Needs review" tripped us up: posts existed but did not show in "Your content" because of a hidden status filter. Make status obvious and default the view to show everything.
2.  **Inline chip handling.** The source-reference chips injected into every text-post draft had to be hand-deleted, and deleting them clipped adjacent words. Render them as removable footnotes, or auto-strip on approval.
3.  **Precise reel controls.** A real trim (we landed at 10.05s, just over a 10s limit) and caption timing control (one caption overlapped the next on a transition).  
    

**The interactive-story engine**

1.  **Honest branching.** Right now, both choices quietly converge back to the main line, which misleads anyone designing a real decision tree. Either support true branches with distinct outcomes, or make the convergence explicit in the editor so people design for it.
2.  **A device-screen asset slot.** For any shot with a monitor or phone, let users supply the screen content as its own image that the Studio composites onto the device. This solves the "text is on the back of the monitor" and garbling problems in one move.

# **Glossary**

| **Term** | **Meaning** |
| --- | --- |
| Human factor | The role people play in security. Trust, helpfulness, habit and time pressure, which attackers target instead of, or before, the technology. |
| Phishing | A fraudulent message that pretends to be from a trusted source to trick the reader into clicking a link, opening a file or giving up information. |
| Spear phishing | A phishing message tailored to a specific person, using details about them or their colleagues to look convincing. |
| Social engineering | Manipulating people into acting against their own interest, for example by using authority, urgency or familiarity. |
| Lookalike domain | A web or email address that imitates a real one, for example desert-oi1-hr.com with the digit 1 in place of the letter l, so it reads as "oil" at a glance. |
| Pretext | The believable story an attacker builds around a message, such as an Eid bonus from HR, to make the request feel normal. |
| Urgency (pressure tactic) | A false deadline or threat added to a message to stop the reader from pausing to check. |
| Credential harvesting | Stealing usernames and passwords, often through a fake login page that looks like the real one. |
| Incident reporting | Telling the security team about a suspicious message or event quickly, so they can warn others and block the source. |
| SCADA / operational technology | The control systems that run physical equipment such as pipelines and valves at a production site. |
| Awareness campaign | A planned set of content that teaches safer behaviour to the people in an organization. |
| Interactive (branching) story | A story where the audience makes a choice and sees the consequence, rather than only reading or watching. |
| Steering note | The free text added at the Studio's Review step to guide the wording and focus of a generated post. |
| Negative prompt | A list of things the Studio must not include, used to stop recurring errors such as a hat on The Ghost or misspelled text. |

# **Conclusion**

The Desert Oil and Gas Campaign delivers all five required asset types as one consistent world: five image posts, six text posts, two reels, one graphic and one interactive story.  
  
Rather than listing security rules, the campaign dramatizes a single realistic human factor attack and drives a single behaviour: when a message feels off or rushes you, ask before you act, and report it.

The build was iterative. Most of the effort went into keeping the Studio on message: holding the characters and palette consistent, keeping on-screen text large and correctly spelled, removing the reference chips from text posts, and trimming the interactive story so it stayed focused.  
  
The prompts and tweaks recorded above are the working method that got each asset to a usable state, and the challenges table is a reusable checklist for the next campaign.
